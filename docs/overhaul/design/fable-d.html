Teaching an agent to listen to 400 support calls a day

Our QA team could sample a handful of the day’s calls. The agent hears all of them. Notes on rubrics, timeouts, and what “listening” means.

Aug 20269 min read python AI agents

A travel company’s support line takes hundreds of calls a day. Quality assurance, done by people, means sampling: a reviewer picks a call, listens end to end, scores it against a checklist, writes it up. Forty minutes of audio takes about forty minutes to hear. On a good day a small team covers a sliver of what happened, and which sliver depends on who felt like clicking what.

So I built an agent to do the listening. It reviews hundreds of customer support calls daily, accomplishing in a single day what would take a team of humans several days — and with a consistency humans simply can’t match. That last clause is the interesting one, and I’ll come back to it.

The pipeline

The architecture is deliberately boring. Boring is what you want when the output can affect how someone’s work is judged.

  1. Pull the day’s call recordings and their ticket context from the helpdesk.
  2. Transcribe the audio — bilingual, code-switching mid-sentence, on a phone line.
  3. Evaluate the transcript against a written rubric, with the order history alongside it.
  4. Emit a report a human can challenge: every score cites the line it heard.

The shape of the review step, roughly:

review.pypython
# The rubric is the real product. The model is a commodity;
# an agreed definition of a good call is not.
RUBRIC = [
    "greeting_and_verification",
    "issue_understood_correctly",
    "policy_quoted_accurately",
    "action_matches_promise",   # the one humans miss most
    "tone_under_pressure",
]

def review(call: Call) -> Verdict:
    transcript = transcribe(call.audio, deadline=8 * MINUTES)
    # An SDK timeout is a request, not a promise. The watchdog
    # kills and re-queues anything that stalls past the deadline.
    evaluation = judge(
        transcript,
        rubric=RUBRIC,
        context=order_history(call.order_id),
    )
    return Verdict(
        call=call.id,
        scores=evaluation.scores,
        evidence=evaluation.quotes,  # every score cites its line
    )

Two of those comments were earned the hard way. The transcription SDK accepts a timeout parameter and, under certain failure modes, ignores it — a run would sit at Transcribing call audio forever while the queue backed up behind it. The fix wasn’t clever: an external watchdog that treats any stall as a failure and re-queues the work. Trust boundaries apply to your own dependencies too.

What the rubric taught me

I expected the model to be the hard part. It wasn’t. The hard part was the rubric — sitting with the QA team and turning “we know a good call when we hear one” into criteria precise enough that two readers, human or machine, reach the same verdict. Every ambiguity we left in the document came back as a dispute later.

  • Judgment is mostly definition. Once “did the action match the promise” was written down carefully, scoring it stopped being interesting. The disagreement had lived in the vagueness.
  • Evidence beats scores. Nobody accepts a number from a machine. Everybody engages with a quoted sentence from their own call. Citations turned the report from a verdict into a conversation.
  • Consistency is the actual superpower. The agent scores call number 380 with exactly the attention it gave call number 1. No human reviewer can say that, and no human should be embarrassed by it — boredom is not a character flaw, it’s a property of consciousness that the agent simply doesn’t have.

Does it listen?

Here is the part I keep turning over. The agent processes every call and misses none. It never has a bad day, never brings its last argument into this one, never tunes out at minute thirty-four. By any operational definition, it is the best listener the team has ever had. And yet “listening,” as people use the word, seems to mean something more than lossless intake — something like attention that costs the attender something.

The question of whether Machines Can Think… is about as relevant as the question of whether Submarines Can Swim.

— Edsger W. Dijkstra, EWD898

Dijkstra’s point cuts both ways. The submarine really does cross the ocean; the agent really does the QA. Whether that counts as swimming, or listening, is a question about our words, not about the machine’s performance. As an engineer I can leave it there. As a person I can’t quite, which is probably why the evidence citations exist: they are institutionalized doubt, the system admitting that its own understanding should be checked against the recording.

There’s something magical about teaching machines to understand context and take action. I wrote that on my About page and I stand by it — with the footnote that “understand” is carrying a lot of weight in that sentence, and I intend to keep an eye on it.
