Teaching an agent to listen to 400 support calls a day

Sastaticket’s support floor takes hundreds of calls a day — refunds, reschedules, a passenger at the wrong counter in the wrong city. Every one of those calls is a promise made on the company’s behalf. Until recently, we could check almost none of them.

The math never worked

A careful human reviewer gets through maybe thirty calls in a shift, and only a fraction of those with real attention. Reviewing what the floor produces in one day would take a team of people several days — so QA wasn’t a process, it was a lottery. Most calls were simply never heard twice.

What the agent actually does

The system I built reviews the whole day’s calls, every day, with high accuracy. It is not one clever prompt. It is a pipeline, and every stage is boring on purpose:

  • Pull the day’s recordings, with the order context attached to each one.
  • Transcribe them. Our calls slide between Urdu and English mid-sentence, so this step decides everything downstream.
  • Evaluate each transcript against a rubric — greeting, identity verification, the accuracy of what the agent promised, tone under pressure.
  • Attach evidence. Every flag carries the exact quote it is based on, so a human can check the machine’s work in seconds.
  • Compile the day into one report the QA team reads with their morning tea.

That’s the whole shape of it. The agent does in one day what would take a team of humans several days — and it never gets tired of call number 400.

There’s something magical about teaching machines to understand context and take action ✨

Consistency is the feature

Accuracy gets the headlines, but consistency is what changed the floor. A human reviewer on Friday afternoon is not the reviewer they were on Monday morning. The agent applies the same rubric to call 1 and call 400, with a consistency humans simply can’t match. An agent flagged twice in one week is now a signal, not an anecdote.

If you’re building one

Transcription is the foundation — every rupee spent there pays for itself downstream. Rubric design turned out to be product work, not prompt work; the interesting arguments were with the QA leads about what “verified” means, not with the model. And keep people in the loop: the agent surfaces, humans decide. That division of labour is why the team trusts it.
