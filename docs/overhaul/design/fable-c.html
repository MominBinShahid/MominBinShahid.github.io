Teaching an agent to listen to 400 support calls a day

2026-06-10 9 min read python
a call, becoming a verdict fig. 01

Our support team takes hundreds of calls a day. Before this project, quality review meant a small group of human reviewers sampling a handful of them: listen to a recording, fill in a scorecard, move to the next. On a good day they covered a few percent of the queue. Everything else went unheard.

I spent the better part of a year building the thing that hears the rest: an agent that reviews every call, every day, against the same rubric a human reviewer would use. This is roughly how it works, and what building it taught me.

stage 01Listen

Everything downstream depends on the transcript, and a transcript is a lossy artifact. Real support calls have crosstalk, hold music, a customer on a motorbike in traffic, and conversations that switch between Urdu and English mid-sentence. The first job is honest transcription with speaker labels and timestamps. The second, less obvious job is knowing when the transcript can’t be trusted — a review built on a garbled transcript is worse than no review, because it arrives wearing the costume of a fact.

So the pipeline’s first verdict is about itself: is this audio good enough to judge? If not, the call is routed out, flagged, and counted. Skipped work you can see is fine. Skipped work you can’t see is how trust dies.

stage 02Read

The rubric lives in code and is versioned like an API, because it is an API — the contract between what the business means by “a good call” and what the agent checks. Each section is evaluated independently: greeting, verification, the actual resolution, closing. And every verdict must quote its evidence back from the transcript. A verdict without a quote is an opinion, and the agent is not paid for opinions.

The evaluation loop itself is almost embarrassingly plain. That’s deliberate: all of the difficulty lives in the rubric prompts and in the plumbing around failure, not in clever orchestration.

review.py python 
RUBRIC = load_rubric("support-call.v4")

async def review(call: Call) -> Review:
    transcript = await transcribe(call.audio)      # stage 01 — listen
    if not transcript.trustworthy:
        return Review.skipped(call.id, reason=transcript.reason)

    findings = []
    for section in RUBRIC.sections:               # stage 02 — read
        verdict = await evaluate(section, transcript)
        assert verdict.evidence, "no quote, no verdict"
        findings.append(verdict)

    return Review(                                # stage 03 — judge
        call_id=call.id,
        score=score(findings),
        findings=findings,
    )

stage 03Judge

Here is the uncomfortable thing I learned while calibrating: two trained human reviewers scoring the same call disagree more often than anyone likes to admit, and the same reviewer disagrees with themselves depending on whether it’s the third call of the morning or the thirtieth of the afternoon. The agent brings something humans structurally cannot: the four-hundredth call of the day is scored with exactly the same patience as the first.

Accomplishing in a single day what would take a team of humans several days, and with a consistency humans simply can’t match.

— the claim I now get to stand behind

That is not a knock on the human reviewers. They moved up the stack: instead of listening to a 3% sample, they now audit the agent — spot-checking its verdicts, arguing with its rubric, catching the cases where it’s confidently wrong. The humans review the reviewer. That loop is the actual product.

appendixWhat I’d tell you before you build one

  • No quote, no verdict. Force the agent to cite the transcript span behind every judgment. It converts hallucination from a silent failure into a visible one.
  • Version the rubric like an API. When scores shift, you need to know whether the calls changed or the judge did.
  • Budget for the transcript lying. Measure transcription quality as its own metric, and let the pipeline decline to judge.
  • Keep humans reviewing the reviewer. Consistency is not the same thing as correctness; you need people auditing the agent to tell them apart.
  • Long audio does strange things to SDKs. Own your timeouts. Trust no default.

I used to say I love building things that live on the web. Some of those things now listen, read, and judge. Teaching machines to understand context and take action still feels like magic ✨ — but it’s the boring engineering above that makes the magic hold.
