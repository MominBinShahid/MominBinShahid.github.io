Teaching an agent to listen to 400 support calls a day

aug 30, 2026  ·  8 min read  ·  •python

The support floor at a travel company is loud in a specific way. Hundreds of calls a day: reschedules, refunds, a flight that moved by forty minutes, somebody’s mother flying alone for the first time. Every one of those calls is a promise made on behalf of the company — and until recently, we could check almost none of them.

QA worked the way it works everywhere. A small team samples a handful of calls, scores them against a rubric, and files the scores days after the conversation happened. Honest work, but a keyhole. So we made a bet: an agent that listens to every call, every day, and shows its evidence.

What listening actually means

A phone line in Karachi is not a podcast studio. Calls code-switch between Urdu and English mid-sentence, agents talk over customers, and half of every recording is hold music at 8 kHz. The transcription layer has exactly one job: produce a diarised transcript faithful enough to quote. We spent more time on that layer than on anything you would call AI.

The design decision that mattered came after transcription: facts before judgement. The agent first extracts what verifiably happened — was the customer greeted, what was promised, did the agent read back the booking reference — and only then scores the call. Ask a model to “rate this call out of five” and you get vibes. Vibes are not QA.

The rubric is the product

The scoring rubric wasn’t mine to invent. The QA team already had one, sharpened by years of actually listening. Our job was to encode it, section by section, and then hold the agent to a standard human reviewers are rarely held to:

Every score must cite its evidence. If the reviewer can’t point at the sentence in the transcript, the score doesn’t count.

That one rule did more for accuracy than any prompt trick. It turns “the agent was polite” into “the agent said this, at 02:41” — which a human can verify in ten seconds. The shape of a review, heavily simplified:

SAMPLE_RATE = 8_000                          # phone-line audio, not studio audio
RUBRIC = load_rubric("qa/rubric.yaml")      # the QA team’s, not mine

async def review(call: CallAudio) -> Review:
    transcript = await transcribe(call)     # diarised, urdu + english
    facts = extract_facts(transcript)       # greetings, promises, read-backs
    scores = [
        section.apply(facts, transcript)    # every score carries a citation
        for section in RUBRIC
    ]
    return Review(call.id, scores,
                  evidence=[s.citation for s in scores])

What broke along the way

  • Code-switched speech. Early transcripts turned perfectly polite Urdu into confident nonsense. Fixing the ears mattered more than upgrading the brain.
  • Long calls versus context windows. A forty-minute call, holds included, does not fit anywhere comfortably. We learned to segment and summarise without ever losing the quotable evidence — the citation has to survive compression.
  • Agreement, not accuracy. There is no ground truth for “was this call handled well”, only the QA team’s judgement. So we measured agreement with human reviewers and kept calibrating until the disagreements were the interesting kind — the ones where a human, shown the evidence, changed their mind about half the time.
  • Cost. Reviewing hundreds of calls daily only works if a review costs less than the tea trolley. Cheap models everywhere the rubric permits; the expensive one only where judgement is genuinely hard.

Where the humans went

Nobody was replaced by this thing. The QA team stopped being samplers and became editors: they spot-check the agent, own the rubric, and handle appeals. The agent does in one day what would take a team of humans several days — and with a consistency humans simply can’t match. The humans, meanwhile, do the one thing only humans can: decide what good means this quarter, and change the rubric when the answer changes.

That trade — machines for coverage, humans for judgement — is the whole trick. There’s something magical about teaching machines to understand context and take action ✨, but the magic only holds if a person can always say to the machine: show me the sentence.
