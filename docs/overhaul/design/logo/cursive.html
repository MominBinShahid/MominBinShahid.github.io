Momin, in hand

Five cursive treatments of Momin, then five ways of fusing the Latin with the Urdu. Nastaliq and Latin script are both calligraphic hands, so they pair naturally — that is the idea the hybrids are testing. Script as shape only; nothing religious.

Five fusions

Latin and Urdu in one mark. Each takes a different approach to the join.

H1Split
Mominمومن
Vertical split

One mark, cut down the middle — Latin signature on the left, Nastaliq on the right, a hairline where they meet. The most literal reading of "half one, half the other".

H2Stacked
Mominمومن
Stacked bilingual

Monoline Latin over Nastaliq, width-matched, a rule between. The calmest and most usable — this is the one that could actually sit in a site header.

H3Swash
Momin مومن
Urdu as the swash

The brush script's descending flourish becomes a line that carries the Urdu beneath it. Reads as a signature with its own translation tucked under the tail.

H4Meem initial
omin
Meem initial

The Urdu م stands in for the capital M and the rest of the name continues in Latin cursive. Reads as "Momin" to everyone; only you see where the M came from.

H5Diagonal
Mominمومن
Diagonal cut

A slanted slice through the mark, Latin above, Urdu below. The slope echoes the way Nastaliq itself descends left — the most dynamic of the five and the hardest to use small.

My picks: H2 Stacked is the one that would genuinely work in a header — calm, legible both ways, no tricks. H4 Meem initial is the cleverest: the mark carries the idea without announcing it. H3 Swash is the most beautiful and the least practical.

Of the five hands, C5 Monoline survives small sizes best and pairs with a geometric sans; C1 Signature has the most authority but needs room; C2 Felt tip is the most personal and the least formal.

All of these are typeset for comparison. Whichever you pick would be redrawn as outlines for production, so the mark stops depending on a webfont loading.
